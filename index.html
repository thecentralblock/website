Where This Started

Two needs, one solution

Women leaving treatment need a home. Downtown needs life.

Problem One

Women finish treatment with too few places to go.

Rice County has far fewer sober housing beds for women than it needs. Women do the hardest work of their lives in treatment, walk out the door, and too often land right back in the circumstances that brought them there. Without stable housing, relapse becomes far more likely.

Problem Two

A historic downtown is losing its anchors.

Central Avenue has some of the finest 1880s commercial architecture in southern Minnesota — and storefronts that sit empty. Every vacant building takes foot traffic with it, and the businesses still holding on feel it. A downtown without daily life stops being a downtown.

The Anchor

Restore Recovery

Sober housing for women, built into the center of town instead of tucked away, with work and community just downstairs. A new model for recovery in southern Minnesota.

A home, not a program.

Twenty sober housing beds on the second floor of the Union Block, with shared kitchens, living rooms, and the daily structure recovery needs. The third floor offers the next step: eight two-bedroom apartments for women and their children.

Recovery means taking your life back. Here, women live as neighbors, not patients, and jobs right downstairs give them more than a paycheck: a schedule to keep, skills to build, and a future of their own choosing.

And For Everyone Else

A safe, fun place for young people downtown

Teens and young adults in rural areas abuse substances at higher rates than their urban counterparts. The reason cited most often: nowhere to go and nothing to do.

A gaming hall. A cat rescue you can volunteer at. A makers studio where you can design something and print it the same afternoon. This block actively draws young people toward something — friendships, skill building, job opportunities, and a positive faith-rooted environment that models what a life built on purpose actually looks like.

Our Story

How one phone call on a Faribault sidewalk turned into the restoration of three historic buildings.

How It Started

In 2015, I was walking through downtown Faribault after visiting a friend who owned a building on Central Avenue. As I passed 206 Central, I noticed a sign in the window: “This building is not empty. It’s full of opportunity. Contact the City of Faribault.”

I pulled out my phone and called right there on the sidewalk.

Kim Clausen, the HRA Director at the time, answered. Within minutes, she was there to give me a tour.

The building was in rough shape. Years of neglect, a fire, and water damage had taken their toll.

A lot of people would have walked away. I saw a building worth saving, and the perfect project for a couple of first-timers like us.

I went straight home and told my husband, Tim, all about it. He was just as excited as I was.

From there, we sat down with the HRA, the EDA, and the City of Faribault and worked out a plan. We put in our own savings, the community added its support, and together we set out to bring 206 back to life.

“This building is not empty. It’s full of opportunity.”

Where We Are Today

Today, three connected 1880s buildings on Central Avenue are coming back to life.

Upstairs, there will be apartments for families and individuals, along with sober housing for women through Restore Recovery. At street level, there will be room for 11 local businesses, from storefronts on Central Avenue to smaller office suites.

I design the project myself. Every floor plan, drawing, and rendering you see on this site is my own work, from the first sketch to the finished image.

For Tim and me, this has never been just about old buildings. It’s about the people, and the lifelong relationships we’ve built along the way.

A healthy downtown is where we gather, shop, and get to know each other. We want Faribault’s downtown to be full of that kind of life again.

Restore Recovery

Sober housing for women, built into the heart of downtown instead of tucked away in a neighborhood. It’s a new model for southern Minnesota: a developer and a recovery nonprofit working side by side to save historic buildings, bring life back to Main Street, and put care, work, and community right downstairs.

Hear Her Story

Restore Recovery, in their own words

A home, not a program.

Women leaving treatment need a safe, stable place to live. Without one, relapse becomes far more likely. Sober housing gives them the time, structure, and support to make recovery last.

Restore Recovery is rooted in Christian faith and open to every woman ready to rebuild. Here, she’ll be surrounded by grace, prayer, and people who believe in second chances.

Restore Recovery hopes to offer 20 sober housing beds for women on the second floor of the Union Block. Each woman will have her own bedroom, her own bathroom, and her own walk-in closet, with a shared kitchen and living room and the daily structure that helps rebuild healthy routines and relationships.

The third floor will offer the next step: eight two-bedroom apartments for reuniting families, so mothers and children can come home together without leaving the community that helped them get there.

Recovery means taking your life back. Here, women will live as neighbors, not patients, and jobs right downstairs give them more than a paycheck: a schedule to keep, skills to build, and a future of their own choosing.

The Relationship

Why this works — in both directions

The connection between recovery and community commerce here is not incidental. It is the design. Each side of this ecosystem makes the other more resilient.

What the businesses give recovery
Dignity. Women walk downstairs to run errands or grab lunch like any other neighbor, not as patients or program residents.
Employment. Real jobs just steps from home, during the time it matters most.
Animal therapy on demand. The Crafty Kitty makes a proven therapeutic tool part of an ordinary afternoon, not a scheduled appointment.
What recovery gives the businesses
A built-in community. Residents, their families, and their support networks bring steady foot traffic to every business on the block.
Shared purpose. People who come for cat therapy, smoothies, or gaming understand that spending here means something beyond the transaction.
A spirit the whole block feels. Recovery rooted in faith brings a joy that runs deeper than circumstance, and the whole block feels it.

Ways to Help

Every kind of contribution has a place here

Whether your gift is capital, labor, materials, or expertise — it moves this project closer to the women who are waiting for it and the downtown that is ready for it.

Now gathering pre-commitments

We aren’t collecting any money yet. While the legal and giving structure is finalized, we’re asking partners to tell us what they’d like to contribute: funding, labor, materials, or sponsorship. A pre-commitment isn’t a payment or a binding promise. It simply tells us you’re in, and we’ll follow up personally when it’s time.

Financial Partners

Any amount

Individual gifts, church and faith-based organization support, foundation grants, and mission-aligned investment. Every dollar closes the gap between plans and shovels in the ground.

Skilled Trades

Donated labor

Most needed right now: an architect and a structural engineer. We also welcome electricians, plumbers, framers, finish carpenters, HVAC, and masonry. Donated hours on a project of this size translate directly into dollars that stay in the mission.

Product & Materials

In-kind donation

Manufacturers and suppliers: flooring, fixtures, appliances, lumber, windows, paint, furnishings. Materials donated at any stage reduce the project’s bottom line.

Corporate Sponsors
